# Using Personas in Business Analysis

Let’s be honest: we all want to build the right thing, not just build it right. But how do we make sure that what we’re defining, documenting, and designing is actually going to resonate with the people who’ll be using it? This is where **personas** come in—one of the most powerful tools in the UX and BA toolkit, and yet still wildly underutilized in many teams.

In this article, we’ll explore **how to use personas effectively as a BA**, and how they can help you bridge the gap between user needs and business goals. If you're thinking "this sounds like UX's job", don’t worry—we're not trying to steal anyone’s thunder. We're just making sure that the analysis phase isn’t missing a key ingredient: empathy.

## What *is* a Persona?

A persona is a semi-fictional character created to represent a major user type. Think of it like a stand-in for a real person: someone you can point to and say, “Would *they* understand this feature? Would *they* use it this way?”

But this isn’t about inventing a random user and slapping a name on them. Good personas are rooted in **real user research**—interviews, surveys, analytics—combined and distilled into a relatable format: a name, face, goals, frustrations, behaviors, even a quote that sums them up. It’s this combo of data + storytelling that makes personas so effective.

## Why Should Analysts Care?

Personas aren’t just for designers. As BAs, we’re often the ones translating fuzzy business ideas into actionable requirements. That means we need to understand users just as deeply as UX does—if not more.

Here’s what personas can do for you:

- **Clarify requirements** by grounding them in real user needs.
- **Avoid assumption-based design** ("I think users want this" vs. "Alex needs this because…").
- **Prioritize features** more effectively—what’s a must-have for Persona A might be irrelevant to Persona B.
- **Support stakeholder alignment**—personas create a shared language for discussing users, especially useful when stakeholders think the user is "everyone".

## How to Use Personas in Your Workflow

### 1. During Discovery
If you're kicking off a project, this is the moment to bring personas in. If UX has already created some, great—use them. If not, start small. Even proto-personas (based on stakeholder input and assumptions) are better than nothing, as long as you’re upfront about their limitations.

Tip: Don’t just collect requirements from stakeholders. Ask: *“Who are we solving this for?”* and *“What do they care about?”*

### 2. When Writing User Stories or Requirements
Instead of generic roles like “the user”, attach a persona to your stories:

> _As **Sophia the First-Time Buyer**, I want to compare loan options easily, so that I feel confident making a decision._

This gives context to every feature, and helps devs and testers stay aligned with the real-world use case.

### 3. In Workshops and Alignment Sessions
Use personas in brainstorming or prioritization meetings. A simple question—*“Would this help Maria?”*—can redirect a conversation that’s drifting into feature bloat or internal bias territory.

Print them. Post them. Mention them. Make them *present*.

## Tips for Creating (or Using) Great Personas

- **Focus on goals and behaviors**, not just demographics.
- **Validate with real data** whenever possible—even if it’s just a few interviews.
- **Visuals matter**—people relate more to faces than bullet points.
- **Keep them human**—add quotes, frustrations, day-in-the-life scenarios.
- **Don’t overdo it**—3–5 core personas are usually enough.

And most importantly: **keep them alive**. If personas sit in a folder no one opens, they’re not helping anyone. Revisit them, tweak them, use them to challenge assumptions regularly.

## Wrap-up: Why It Matters

At the end of the day, personas are just another way to answer the question we should be asking at every step: *“Who are we building this for?”*

When you get that answer right—and keep it front and center—everything else gets easier: requirements make more sense, stakeholders stop arguing over “nice-to-haves,” and users… well, users actually like what we deliver.

And that’s the goal, right?
